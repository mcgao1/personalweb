# Effects of Aspartame and Acesulfame Potassium on the Antioxidant Enzyme Activity of the Earthworm Eisenia fetida

*(Chinese title: 阿斯巴甜和安赛蜜对赤子爱胜蚓的毒性效应 / 酶活影响 — Nanjing Agricultural University Bachelor's Thesis, author 高曼宸 (Manchen Gao), supervisor Prof. Sun Mingming (孙明明), College of Resources and Environmental Sciences, Ecology major, submitted April 29, 2024)*

## One-line summary
An undergraduate ecotoxicology thesis testing how two artificial sweeteners (aspartame and acesulfame potassium) affect earthworm (Eisenia fetida) survival and antioxidant enzyme (SOD, CAT) activity, using filter-paper contact and soil-incubation assays.

## Background
Artificial, non-nutritive sweeteners (e.g., aspartame/ASP, acesulfame potassium/ACE, sucralose, saccharin, cyclamate) are FDA-approved, widely used in food, beverages, and even livestock feed, and are poorly metabolized by animals and humans — so they are excreted largely intact and persist in the environment. China is reported as the world's largest consumer of these sweeteners. They have been detected as emerging contaminants in pig-farm wastewater, surface water, groundwater, drinking water, soil, and even air, with reported soil concentrations in North/South China ranging roughly from sub-µg/kg to several hundred µg/kg dry weight, and estimated soil loading rates on the order of tens of grams per hectare per year near livestock operations. The literature review documents effects on gut microbiota and antibiotic-resistance-gene transfer in bacteria, and toxicity in aquatic organisms (fish, water fleas) and mammals, but notes that data on soil invertebrates — and earthworms specifically — are very limited. The thesis frames this gap as its motivation: to use Eisenia fetida (red earthworm / red wiggler), a standard soil bioindicator species, to gain preliminary insight into the ecological risk that aspartame and acesulfame pose to soil ecosystems.

## What she did (methodology)
The study combined two complementary experiments.

**1. Filter-paper contact (acute toxicity) experiment.** Following OECD Guideline No. 207, Eisenia fetida individuals (350–450 mg body weight, sourced from an earthworm farm in Jurong, Jiangsu, and acclimated 15 days in cow-manure soil) were each placed alone in a Petri dish lined with filter paper dosed with a sweetener solution (aspartame alone, acesulfame alone, or a 1:1 mixture), at 5 concentration levels per treatment (0, 25, 250, 500, 2500, 5000 mg/mL nominal dosing solution, corresponding to roughly 0–78 mg/cm² on the paper) plus a control, with 10 replicates (one worm each) per concentration — 15 treatment groups plus control. Worms were kept at 20±1°C, 80% humidity, in the dark, checked every 6 hours, and survival/mortality/intoxication symptoms were recorded at 24 h and 48 h. This pre-experiment was used to identify a lethal concentration range and to set the doses for the soil experiment.

**2. Soil incubation experiment.** Based on the filter-paper results (and the observation that environmentally relevant soil concentrations are far lower), four soil concentrations — 0.024, 2.4, 24, and 240 mg/kg dry soil — were tested for each sweetener (ACE and ASP) separately, each with 5 replicates, plus a cow-manure-soil control, over 14 days. Soil moisture was maintained at 25–35%; mortality was recorded at days 3, 7, and 14. At the end of the 14-day exposure, surviving worms were anesthetized, dissected to remove gut contents, and the remaining tissue was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and homogenized for enzyme assays. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was measured via a WST-8 colorimetric kit (450 nm absorbance) and catalase (CAT) activity via an ammonium-molybdate colorimetric method (405 nm), both using commercial kits (Comin Biotechnology, Suzhou) and a microplate reader. Data were processed in Excel, analyzed for statistical significance in SPSSPRO/RStudio (Kruskal-Wallis test), and plotted with Chiplot.

## Results
- **Filter-paper acute toxicity:** At high sweetener concentrations, survival dropped sharply relative to control (roughly 40–100% lower survival, p<0.05), with worms showing curling, lesions, clitellum swelling, and eventually death with strong odor and tissue degradation. Notably, at lower concentrations, survival in some sweetener-treated groups was *higher* than in the control (p<0.05) — interpreted as a possible antibiotic-like/hormetic effect at low doses.
- **Soil mortality (14-day):** Mortality was low in all groups for the first 3 days, then rose. The control group had significantly higher survival than all sweetener-treated groups overall (p<0.05). Both the 0.024 mg/kg and 240 mg/kg treatments (the lowest and highest concentrations) tended to show relatively higher mortality than the intermediate concentrations, broadly consistent with the filter-paper results.
- **SOD activity:** Kruskal-Wallis test significant overall (P = 0.002, Cohen's f = 0.686). Control SOD activity was roughly 140–200 U/g (fresh weight). Acesulfame (ACE) treatment increased SOD activity in a dose-dependent manner, up to ~320 U/g at 240 mg/kg — significantly higher than control. Aspartame (ASP) treatment showed a more complex, non-monotonic pattern: SOD activity decreased then increased as concentration decreased, opposite to the ACE trend. Overall, ACE-treated worms had higher average SOD activity than ASP-treated worms.
- **CAT activity:** Kruskal-Wallis test also significant (P = 0.003, Cohen's f = 0.669). CAT activity trends across ACE concentrations were fairly consistent/monotonic-ish, while the ASP group showed an inconsistent pattern (notably the lowest ASP concentration, 0.024 mg/kg, had lower CAT than other ASP groups) — but all sweetener-treated groups had higher CAT than control (p<0.05). ACE-treated worms had higher average CAT activity than ASP-treated worms.
- The thesis interprets ACE's more consistent, dose-dependent stimulation of both enzymes as evidence of a clearer/stronger stressor effect on E. fetida biochemistry, while ASP's inconsistent pattern is attributed to speculative causes (its methanol metabolite, differing water solubility of ASP vs. ACE affecting worm uptake, possible measurement/instrument variability, or a more complex physiological interaction).

## Conclusion
Both aspartame and acesulfame appear to exert some biological toxicity toward Eisenia fetida: at low concentrations they may behave somewhat like antibiotics and slightly aid survival, while at higher concentrations they impair survival and provoke an antioxidant enzyme (SOD/CAT) response. Acesulfame's effects on both enzymes were more consistent and more clearly dose-dependent than aspartame's, suggesting its physicochemical properties (e.g., high water solubility, persistence) make it more bioavailable/impactful to the worms. The author frames SOD and CAT activity as candidate biomarkers of oxidative stress from sweetener exposure in soil, offering preliminary data for future ecological risk assessment of these emerging contaminants, while explicitly flagging limitations: soil's buffering capacity may mask true toxicity, enzyme activity is a noisy/unreliable single biomarker (comet assay/DNA damage suggested as a more sensitive future measure), and the study only examined single sweeteners rather than realistic mixtures. Future work suggested: testing other soil invertebrates (springtails, nematodes), studying sweetener transformation/metabolite pathways in soil, bioaccumulation/biomagnification in soil food chains, and improved environmental monitoring/regulation of sweeteners.

## Open questions
- **CV vs. thesis framing mismatch (flagged explicitly):** The CV describes this work as "Effects of Artificial Sweeteners on Earthworm Gut Microbiota" with methods "ran OECD soil-exposure experiments and analysed 16S rRNA sequencing data on how earthworm gut microbiota respond to soil contaminants." The actual thesis (both the PDF title page and abstract) is titled around "毒性效应" (toxicity effects) / "酶活影响" (enzyme activity effects), and its actual methodology is OECD filter-paper acute toxicity testing plus a soil incubation experiment measuring **SOD and CAT antioxidant enzyme activity** via colorimetric enzyme kits — there is no 16S rRNA sequencing or gut microbiota community analysis anywhere in the thesis body, results, or conclusion. The thesis does collect earthworm gut contents during dissection ("获取肠道内容物"/"用于测定氧化应激指标"), but the samples are described as being frozen and used for the oxidative-stress (enzyme) measurements, not for microbiome sequencing. The literature review (background section) does discuss gut microbiota effects of sweeteners in other studies (e.g., cited work on ACE effects on earthworm gut microbiota, Lin et al. 2024) as motivation/context, but this is a review of prior published work, not something Gao performed herself. **Recommendation: the CV description appears to overstate/misdescribe the actual thesis work — it should probably be revised to reflect the enzyme-activity/oxidative-stress focus (SOD, CAT) and acute-toxicity design actually used, unless a gut-microbiota/16S component exists in a separate part of the project not captured in this PDF or the draft docx files (worth confirming with Manchen directly).**
