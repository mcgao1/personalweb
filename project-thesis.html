MASTER'S THESIS

Investment Feasibility of BIPV Across High-Rise Clusters in Kunming

Does building-integrated solar actually pay off? It depends on one variable most people ignore.

Project designer & lead researcher (independent) · Dec 2025 – Jul 2026

Background

This was my master's thesis. Building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) is often pitched as the answer for decarbonising dense cities, but nobody had systematically quantified what happens to the investment case when tall buildings sit close together and shade each other. That's exactly the situation in Kunming's high-rise office clusters.

What I did

  1. Modelled four real high-rise clusters in central Kunming (including Hanglong, Qicai, Tongde and Wanda) in 3D and ran solar-radiation simulations to find out how much light each building actually receives once mutual shading from its neighbours is accounted for, not just the light it would get standing alone.
  1. Built a financial model (DCF/NPV) comparing two deployment strategies (covering every available roof surface versus only the best-lit sections) across 24+ urban-form scenarios, so the numbers reflect real cluster geometry rather than a single idealised building.
  1. Ran a Monte Carlo simulation to quantify investment risk, plus a subsidy-threshold sensitivity analysis to find the tipping point where each scenario turns profitable.
The "sweet spot" flips it. Only deploying panels where they actually pay off turns most loss-making scenarios into profitable ones, and roof-utilisation ends up mattering more than which PV panels get chosen.

Result

Treating the radiation cutoff itself as something to optimise, deploying panels only where they actually pay off, flips most scenarios from a loss into a profit. The analysis also shows that roof-utilisation is a bigger driver of investment risk than the choice of PV technology itself, meaning decisions made at the architectural-design stage matter more than which panels get installed later.

In practical terms, this means BIPV investment decisions can't be separated from the massing decisions made earlier in the design process: how tall, how close, how oriented. A financially attractive design in isolation can become a losing one once its neighbours are added to the model, and vice versa. That's the part urban planning and green-building policy tend to overlook when they treat solar potential building-by-building instead of cluster-by-cluster.

The subsidy-sensitivity analysis shows how close some scenarios sit to the break-even line. A small shift in incentive policy is enough to flip several borderline cases from viable to not, which is exactly the kind of threshold effect that's easy to miss when it's only ever assessed one building at a time.
